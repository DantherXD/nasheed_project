The Nasheed Library

A sourced database and aggregation map of Islamic vocal music: the munshideen who sing it, the a cappella and percussion-only tradition first, the labels and platforms that release it, and the exact APIs and catalogues you can keep extending it from.

What this library counts — and what it deliberately separates

A nasheed (نشيد, plural أناشيد anāshīd) is Islamic devotional song. The word is modern, broad and media-friendly; practitioners and scholars usually use more precise terms. This database keeps them distinct so a search doesn't collapse a Sufi ibtihāl, a Malay nasyid group into one undifferentiated bucket.

The single most common error in English-language coverage is treating a Qur'an reciter (qāri') as a munshid. Many great munshideen were also reciters — al-Naqshabandi, al-Fajni, alafasy — but the two repertoires and their rules are different. Records here are tagged reciter when nasheed is the secondary output.

Vocal-only vs instrumental

The instrumentation field is the most consequential one in the database, because it decides whether an item counts as nasheed for a given community. Four positions are in play:

  • Voice only. The strictest position, associated with Salafi-leaning scholarship: no instruments, no imitated instruments, and no vocal rhythms made to sound like instruments.
  • Voice + duff only. The frame drum is the classic exception across the schools; most scholars still exclude everything else, including the darbuka.
  • Percussion ensemble. Wider drum sets, as in Malaysian nasyid and Persian maddāḥī.
  • Instrumental or orchestral. The modern industry norm since Sami Yusuf's Al-Mu'allim (2003) and Maher Zain's Thank You Allah (2009). Many labels now ship a parallel “vocals only” version of the same album.

This library defaults to vocal-first ordering and has a one-click filter for a cappella and duff-only records, since that is the tradition most people mean when they ask for nasheed “without music”.

Regional traditions worth knowing

  • Egypt: the ibtihāl school — Ali Mahmoud → al-Fashni → al-Naqshabandi → Tobar — plus a living Sufi inshād scene around Yasin al-Tohamy and, since 2017, all-women ensembles.
  • Aleppo and Damascus: family madīḥ ensembles, religious muwashshaḥ and daily dhikr; the Abu Sha'r brothers are the best-known export.
  • Gulf: cassette-era “committed” nasheed, then the 2000s video-nasheed wave (Mishary al-Arada, Alafasy), then the pop crossover (Humood, Maher Zain).
  • Sudan and Mauritania: madīḥ dynasties (Awlad Hajj al-Mahi, Awlad al-Bura'i) and the Hassaniya medh tradition — two tar drums, no melody instruments.
  • Morocco: samā' and madīḥ inside the Andalusian nūba world, plus a commercial Darija nasheed market.
  • South Asia: the na'at khawān tradition in Urdu, the enormous Malayalam Mappila industry, Bengali gojol, Tamil Islamic devotional songs.
  • Southeast Asia: Nada Murni (1975) → Raihan (1996) → Rabbani, Hijjaz, In-Team, UNIC; in Indonesia, Snada made a cappella nasyid the default, then Haddad Alwi's Cinta Rasul sold it to the mainstream.
  • Turkey and Iran: ilahi inside Ottoman classical music; maddāḥī as a mass devotional form — both largely instrumental, the opposite pole from Gulf a cappella.
  • East Africa: Swahili qaswida with duff choirs in Zanzibar, Dar es Salaam, Mombasa and Lamu.

Five historical waves

  • Pre-1925: mawlid and qasīda in zawiyas and mosques; the munshid as a role inside an ensemble.
  • 1905–1950: the gramophone era — Salama Higazi cuts 50+ Odeon records; Cairo radio and the 1932 Congress institutionalise inshād.
  • 1970s–1990s: the cassette boom. Gulf and Levantine a cappella nasheed, Pakistani na'at on Heera and Oriental Star, Malaysian nasyid as pop.
  • 2003–2015: Awakening and the orchestral/English crossover; YouTube turns nasheed into a global video genre.
  • 2015–now: streaming-native munshideen, vocals-only channels and touring circuits, copyright-free nasheed libraries, and AI-assisted production.

How to read confidence

Every record carries a dot: high means the artist and at least some works were confirmed against a label page, institutional page or reliable reference; medium means the artist is confirmed but the discography or dates are partial; low means the name is attested but the details are unverified — treat those as research leads, not facts. Roughly a fifth of this database is medium or low by design, because inventing a plausible album title is worse than admitting a gap.

Known gaps (be honest about the map)

  • Latin America and the Caribbean: seven records now, from Caribbean qaseeda to Argentine Sufi ensembles; Mexico, Brazil, Chile and the Andes remain blank.
  • Central Asia: thirteen records across Tajik maddoh, Uzbek na'at and Azerbaijani mugham; Turkmenistan, Georgia and Armenia yielded nothing.
  • Balkans: Bosnian, Sandžak and Macedonian-Albanian ilahija now covered; Albania, Bulgaria, Montenegro and Romania remain undocumented.
  • East Asia: seven records — Korean and Japanese convert-nasheed, Uyghur zikr, Cham ariya, one Mongolian field recording.
  • Europe: Germany, France, the Netherlands, Spain, Italy and Denmark have records; Belgium, Sweden, Norway, Ireland and Austria do not.
  • Brunei, Fiji and Papua New Guinea: nasyid is documented as present, with no named artists verifiable.

Rules this library follows

  • Metadata and links only. No audio is hosted here. Spotify's developer terms forbid building a database from its content; YouTube forbids storing audiovisual data and caps metadata retention at 30 days; Deezer forbids audio storage. So the compliant pattern is index → link out → embed with the platform's own player.
  • A Creative Commons tag on a re-upload is not a licence. It must come from the rights holder. Only CC0, CC BY and CC BY-SA are safe for broad reuse; NC blocks ad-supported use and ND blocks syncing to video.
  • Extremist content is excluded. A meaningful slice of what is tagged “nasheed” on open archives is ISIS or Taliban propaganda; hosting or surfacing it carries platform and legal exposure well beyond copyright, so it needs a denylist and a takedown process from day one.
  • Political chant is flagged, not merged. Revolutionary and partisan chant (Syria 2011, Yemen, Iraq) is catalogued as adjacent where the source classifies the performer as a munshid.
