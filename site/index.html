How to read a record

Every artist carries a confidence dot. high means the artist and at least some works were confirmed against a label, institutional or reliable reference. medium means the artist is confirmed but the discography or dates are partial. low means the name is attested but the details are unverified — treat those as research leads, not facts.

No album title, date or label was invented to fill a cell. Where a field could not be verified it says so or is left blank.

Instrumentation decides whether a record counts as nasheed for a given community, so it is a first-class field, not a footnote. acap is voice only, duff is voice plus frame drum, and the vocal-only toggle restricts the roster to exactly those two.

What this library counts — and what it separates

A nasheed (نشيد, plural أناشيد anāshīd) is Islamic devotional song. The word is modern, broad and media-friendly; practitioners and scholars usually use more precise terms. This database keeps them distinct so a search doesn't collapse a Sufi ibtihāl and a Malay nasyid group into one undifferentiated bucket.

The single most common error in English-language coverage is treating a Qur'an reciter (qāri') as a munshid. Many great munshideen were also reciters — al-Naqshabandi, al-Fashni, alafasy — but the two repertoires and their rules are different. Records here are tagged reciter when nasheed is the secondary output.

Vocal-only vs instrumental

The instrumentation field is the most consequential one in the database, because it decides whether an item counts as nasheed for a given community. Four positions are in play:

  • Voice only. The strictest position, associated with Salafi-leaning scholarship: no instruments, no imitated instruments, and no vocal rhythms made to sound like instruments.
  • Voice + duff only. The frame drum is the classic exception across the schools; most scholars still exclude everything else, including the darbuka.
  • Percussion ensemble. Wider drum sets, as in Malaysian nasyid and Persian maddāḥī.
  • Instrumental or orchestral. The modern industry norm since Sami Yusuf's Al-Mu'allim (2003) and Maher Zain's Thank You Allah (2009). Many labels now ship a parallel “vocals only” version of the same album.

The library defaults to vocal-first ordering and has a one-click filter for a cappella and duff-only records, since that is the tradition most people mean when they ask for nasheed “without music”.

Regional traditions worth knowing

  • Egypt: the ibtihāl school — Ali Mahmoud → al-Fashni → al-Naqshabandi → Tobar — plus a living Sufi inshād scene around Yasin al-Tohamy and, since 2017, all-women ensembles.
  • Aleppo and Damascus: family madīḥ ensembles, religious muwashshaḥ and daily dhikr; the Abu Sha'r brothers are the best-known export.
  • Gulf: cassette-era “committed” nasheed, then the 2000s video-nasheed wave (Mishary al-Arada, Alafasy), then the pop crossover (Humood, Maher Zain).
  • Sudan and Mauritania: madīḥ dynasties (Awlad Hajj al-Mahi, Awlad al-Bura'i) and the Hassaniya medh tradition — two tar drums, no melody instruments.
  • Morocco: samā' and madīḥ inside the Andalusian nūba world, plus a commercial Darija nasheed market.
  • South Asia: the na'at khawān tradition in Urdu, the enormous Malayalam Mappila industry, Bengali gojol and Tamil Islamic devotional songs.
  • Southeast Asia: Nada Murni (1975) → Raihan (1996) → Rabbani, Hijjaz, In-Team, UNIC; in Indonesia, Snada made a cappella nasyid the default, then Haddad Alwi's Cinta Rasul sold it to the mainstream.
  • Turkey and Iran: ilahi inside Ottoman classical music; maddāḥī as a mass devotional form — both largely instrumental, the opposite pole from Gulf a cappella.
  • East Africa: Swahili qaswida with duff choirs in Zanzibar, Dar es Salaam, Mombasa and Lamu.

Five historical waves

  • Pre-1925: mawlid and qaṣīda in zawiyas and mosques; the munshid as a role inside an ensemble.
  • 1905–1950: the gramophone era — Salama Higazi cuts 50+ Odeon records; Cairo radio and the 1932 Congress institutionalise inshād.
  • 1970s–1990s: the cassette boom. Gulf and Levantine a cappella nasheed, Pakistani na'at on Heera and Oriental Star, Malaysian nasyid as pop.
  • 2003–2015: Awakening and the orchestral/English crossover; YouTube turns nasheed into a global video genre.
  • 2015–now: streaming-native munshideen, vocals-only channels and touring circuits, copyright-free nasheed libraries, and AI-assisted production.

How to read confidence

Every record carries a dot. High means the artist and at least some works were confirmed against a label page, institutional page or reliable reference. Medium means the artist is confirmed but the discography or dates are partial. Low means the name is attested but the details are unverified — research leads, not facts. Roughly a fifth of this database is medium or low by design, because inventing a plausible album title is worse than admitting a gap.

Known gaps

  • Latin America and the Caribbean: seven records now — Caribbean qaseeda singing and Argentine Sufi ensembles. Mexico, Brazil, Chile and the Andes remain blank: Spanish- and Portuguese-language nasheed exists and is documented institutionally, but named recording artists do not surface.
  • Central Asia: thirteen records, mostly Tajik maddoh, Uzbek na't and Azerbaijani mugham drawn from Smithsonian and AKDN releases. Turkmenistan, Georgia and Armenia returned nothing verifiable.
  • Balkans: Bosnian, Sandžak and Macedonian-Albanian ilahija are now well covered. Albania itself, Bulgaria's Pomak and Turkish scenes, Montenegro and Romania remain undocumented.
  • East Asia: seven records — Korean and Japanese convert-nasheed, Uyghur zikr masters, Cham ariya and one Mongolian archival recording. Taiwan and Cambodia surfaced nothing creditable.
  • Europe: Germany, France, the Netherlands, Spain, Italy and Denmark now have records. Belgium, Sweden, Norway, Ireland and Austria return touring acts and anonymous self-released streaming profiles with no stated country, which this library will not guess at.
  • Southeast Asia edges: Brunei, Fiji and Papua New Guinea describe nasyid in print but credit no artist.

Rules this library follows

  • Metadata and links only. No audio is hosted here. Spotify's developer terms forbid building a database from its content; YouTube forbids storing audiovisual data and caps metadata retention at 30 days; Deezer forbids audio storage. The compliant pattern is index → link out → embed with the platform's own player.
  • A Creative Commons tag on a re-upload is not a licence. It must come from the rights holder. Only CC0, CC BY and CC BY-SA are safe for broad reuse; NC blocks ad-supported use and ND blocks syncing to video.
  • Extremist content is excluded. A meaningful slice of what is tagged “nasheed” on open archives is ISIS or Taliban propaganda; hosting or surfacing it carries platform and legal exposure well beyond copyright, so it needs a denylist and a takedown process from day one.
  • Political chant is flagged, not merged. Revolutionary and partisan chant (Syria 2011, Yemen, Iraq) is catalogued as adjacent where the source classifies the performer as a munshid.
